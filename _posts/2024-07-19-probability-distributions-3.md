<h4>The Subtle Art of Knowing What You Don’t Know — Part 3: A Closer Look to Likelihood-Prior Relationship</h4><p>In my first article, we talked about some non-parametric density estimation methods and later found out that it might be computationally expensive to approximate all points.</p><p><a href="https://yamaceay.medium.com/probability-distributions-1-8eddc5e434e9">Probability Distributions 1</a></p><p>In my second article, we tackled this problem by using parametric density estimation methods, which — in exchange for additional assumptions about the underlying distribution — offer better generalization and more efficient computation.</p><p><a href="https://yamaceay.medium.com/probability-distributions-2-bac9bbed470f">Probability Distributions 2</a></p><p>In this article, we want to finally learn how to estimate prior distribution given a likelihood estimation, and wrap up everything.</p><p>Let’s say we are given a likelihood estimation. A prior distribution is conjugate to a likelihood distribution, if both the prior and posterior follow the same type of distribution, let’s say F-distribution. So, the belief can be updated only by adjusting the parameters of F. The parameters of F might be some statistics regarding the history of belief updates. The input of F are the parameters of the likelihood functions, which are tested.</p><h4>Binomial and Beta Distribution</h4><p>In order to digest all this, let’s continue the Bernoulli experiment from the previous article, but this time, we want to do multiple (<em>n</em>) games and count the number of red occurrences. So, the likelihood follows a <em>Binomial</em> distribution with the following density function:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*9leoi_YjgZSIEw4_kiYruw.png" /></figure><p>The likelihood of a sequence with <em>k</em> red occurrences is given by the blue part of the formula, and the binomial coefficient in the yellow part tells how many such occurrences exist. <em>Binomial</em> distribution answers how likely <em>k</em> success events happen, given a number of trials <em>n</em> and a success probability <em>p</em>.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/341/1*WzWVIyxS8pSPCnn9AycHPw.gif" /><figcaption>Example Binomial Distribution, <a href="https://stats.stackexchange.com/questions/176425/why-is-a-binomial-distribution-bell-shaped">https://stats.stackexchange.com/questions/176425/why-is-a-binomial-distribution-bell-shaped</a></figcaption></figure><p>Assume that the likelihood following a <em>Binomial</em> distribution accepts a prior / posterior following a <em>Beta</em> distribution.</p><ul><li>Intuitively, this distribution needs two parameters: <em>a</em> and <em>b</em>, tracking the number of past red and black occurrences respectively<em>. </em>So, if the prior follows <em>Beta(P | a, b)</em>, then the posterior after <em>k</em> red and <em>n - k</em> black outcomes must follow <em>Beta(P | a + k, b + (n - k))</em>.</li><li>This distribution must also take the success probability <em>p</em> as input, which is the only variable in the likelihood function to be tested.</li></ul><p>As it turns out, the <em>Beta </em>distribution defined below satisfies this condition:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*9IUMdh3mkM1jXsQ7RQkemg.png" /></figure><p>Notice the correspondence between <em>Beta</em> and <em>Binomial</em> distribution? <em>Γ(a) </em>can be regarded as an analytical continuation of <em>(a - 1)!</em>, and <em>B(a, b)</em> seem to be a pseudo-binomial coefficient, which makes a probability distribution by normalizing the term. Also, you might wonder why we take <em>a-1</em> and <em>b-1</em> and why not <em>a</em> and <em>b</em>?</p><p>Because prior is in fact a way to represent the following information: Given <em>a-1</em> red and <em>b-1</em> black occurrences, when does the next red / black occur?</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/329/1*HFs-ivHyA2vAc_6OlDddUA.png" /><figcaption>Example Beta Distribution, <a href="https://byjus.com/maths/beta-distribution/">https://byjus.com/maths/beta-distribution/</a></figcaption></figure><p>On another note, <em>Beta</em> prior can be used with <em>Bernoulli</em> distribution as well, since it is a special case of <em>Binomial</em> distribution with <em>n = 1</em>. So, a prior can be updated given different types of likelihoods.</p><h4>Geometric Distribution</h4><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/560/1*McF4XuYpSzAkr0iR0dWqkQ.png" /><figcaption>Example Geometric Distribution, <a href="https://de.mathworks.com/help/stats/geometric-distribution.html">https://de.mathworks.com/help/stats/geometric-distribution.html</a></figcaption></figure><p>Can we somehow measure the distance between two success events, given that a variable has a fixed success probability <em>p</em> as in <em>Bernoulli</em> distribution?</p><p>Yes, the distribution is called <em>Geometric</em> distribution and it tells how likely the next success event will happen within <em>n</em> trials, given a success probability <em>p</em>. The formula is pretty self-explanatory:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/656/1*atyu15VSEJvzgZxQp2tXHg.png" /></figure><h4>Poisson, Gamma and Exponential Distribution</h4><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/567/1*c7-8vCve38g5eG62RzNtWQ.png" /></figure><p>Now, we consider another likelihood-prior pair, which is very crucial in neuroscience: <em>Poisson</em> distribution as likelihood and <em>Gamma</em> distribution as prior. As you may recall from the first article, we can count the number of neural spikes in regular time intervals to get a more general estimation of spike rates. Why <em>Poisson</em> distribution (and not <em>Binomial</em> distribution)?</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/300/1*iPGvg9c5TGKhIynar2K6gw.png" /><figcaption>Example Poisson Distribution, <a href="https://calcworkshop.com/discrete-probability-distribution/poisson-distribution/">https://calcworkshop.com/discrete-probability-distribution/poisson-distribution/</a></figcaption></figure><p>Theoretically, if you make the time bins extremely small, so that there are infinitely many bins (<em>n</em>), the success probability (<em>p</em>) of a spike will go near zero as well. Apparently, the spike rate remains constant <em>λ = n · p</em> even with <em>p</em> being infinitely close to zero and <em>n</em> being infinitely large, because the time interval <em>τ </em>is fixed. <em>Poisson</em> distribution is an approximation of <em>Binomial</em> distribution, which is given by the formula below:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/820/1*3QsXH-TQ8uZ-WVB-eybAaw.png" /></figure><p>Now, we’re looking for a <em>Gamma</em> distribution, which tells us the total time <em>∆t </em>for <em>k</em> spikes to happen with an instantaneous rate <em>r</em>. Instantaneous rate <em>r</em> is the slope of the spike rate with respect to time, as given below:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/134/1*iYQJRMy1PQhQZjoBXk1bmg.png" /></figure><p>Remember that <em>Poisson</em> tells us how many spikes we expect in a <strong>fixed</strong> time interval <em>τ</em> with rate <em>λ</em>, so we can rewrite <em>λ = r τ</em>. With this in mind, let’s construct the <em>Gamma</em> distribution step by step.</p><p>At the beginning, imagine a special case of <em>Gamma</em> distribution with <em>k = 1</em>, which should tell us the time between each spike <em>∆t</em> with the same instantaneous rate <em>r</em>.</p><ul><li>It is easy to show that a <em>Poisson(X = 0 | r τ) </em>represents the likelihood that no spike occurs at given time interval <em>τ</em> with an instantaneous spike rate <em>r.</em></li></ul><p>Multiplying this term with the instantaneous spike rate <em>r</em> yields <em>Exponential</em> distribution, which is specified by the following formula:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/500/1*mfP1OnTi_Etf35TVpez28Q.png" /></figure><p>It is not a coincidence that it looks like an analytical continuation of <em>Geometric</em> distribution:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/268/1*xPCgMW3psB4Bf5XOH5JBRA.png" /><figcaption>Example Exponential Distribution, <a href="https://byjus.com/maths/exponential-distribution/">https://byjus.com/maths/exponential-distribution/</a></figcaption></figure><p>Now, let’s define <em>Gamma</em> distribution for arbitrary <em>k </em>values by following the same argumentation:</p><ul><li><em>Poisson(X = k - 1 | r τ) </em>telling how likely will <em>k - 1</em> spikes occur at given spike rate <em>r τ</em>.</li><li><em>Exponential(T = ∆t | r)</em> telling how likely will the <em>k</em>-th spike occur at given time interval <em>∆t</em>.</li></ul><p>Gamma distribution must be the multiplication of both factors, which — after a few intermediate steps — looks like:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/662/1*lnZo4t3Cb1U4vjicYASaww.png" /></figure><p>Given a prior following <em>Gamma(T | k, r)</em> and a new observation with <em>m</em> spikes in <em>n</em> time bins, then the posterior distribution follows <em>Gamma(λ | k + m, r + n)</em>. All in all, we can represent beliefs and perform belief updates using <em>Gamma</em>, when the likelihood distribution is <em>Poisson</em> distributed.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/215/1*nqp7GOdl11dZh3nT0cKKYA.jpeg" /><figcaption>Gamma vs. Poisson Distribution, <a href="https://bcss.org.my/tut/bayes-with-jags-a-tutorial-for-wildlife-researchers/simple-model-with-one-parameter/">https://bcss.org.my/tut/bayes-with-jags-a-tutorial-for-wildlife-researchers/simple-model-with-one-parameter/</a></figcaption></figure><h4>Discussion</h4><p>I hate to say that I took so many detours and explained the content very chaotically.
