<h4>What do an OR-Latch, a game bot and ChatGPT have in common?</h4><figure><img alt="Weirdest Explanation of Memory" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*zT4xuH7dKtn05G7lcForJQ.png" /><figcaption>Illustration of memory. By DALL-E.</figcaption></figure><p>This time, I decided to fit everything memory-related into a single article so that we explore hidden connections between concepts across different fields, be ready for some surprising revelations and a-ha moments!</p><p><em>Memory</em> is an observable property or a function of any system to <em>store</em> data in a <em>reusable</em> way. Ideally, a system memory should persist some info as requested, and response with the “same” info whenever it is queried. Note that we will relax the “sameness” criteria later a bit. Which property of a system memory unit justifies this functionality?</p><p>There are 4 candidates worth considering: Recurrence, recursion, reoccurrence, repetition. They all are so similar that I bet you use them interchangeably on a daily basis. In fact, each term has its specific context and nuance, yet they are appropriate for different situations:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*Skf-E9T0_IM5nib1t2PRhg.png" /><figcaption>Table of definitions. By Yamac Eren Ay.</figcaption></figure><p>In my humble opinion, <em>recurrence</em> explains the best how the memory should operate. Now that we’re done with the necessary evil, let’s start with the most primitive memory models.</p><h4>Memory in Electric Circuits</h4><p>If you didn’t have to take some sort of mandatory “Introduction to Computer Architecture” class during your studies, here are some extra resources to get you started:</p><ul><li><a href="https://youtube.com/playlist?list=PL8dPuuaLjXtNlUrzyH5r6jN9ulIgZBpdo&amp;si=GUXchS73kyJSbktn">First 6 videos in CrashCourse Computer Science playlist explaining how memory is achieved using electric circuits.</a></li><li><a href="https://medium.com/swlh/building-memory-with-logic-gates-f36aec5c11b2">A well-written Medium article explaining how memory can be built using logic gates.</a></li><li><a href="https://diyodemag.com/education/jk_sr_d_flip_flops_latches_nand_nor_terminology">An even more extensive article making a proper introduction.</a></li></ul><p>Now, here is my humble contribution to this topic: everything starts with a simple <em>recurrent</em> connection. Basically, if there was no recurrence, there wouldn’t be memory. Take the following OR-Latch for example: if you <em>set</em> A at any point to true, then the output YES stays true forever. In addition to OR-Latch, SR-Latch allows <em>resetting</em> the memory as well by defining two parallel gates with mutual recurrent connections.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/658/1*ZhCUBfVqpyJkQlOzs4n6Aw.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/648/1*nWlpNeJbU4c0Mpp8DZNudw.png" /><figcaption>Left: OR-Latch, Right: SR-Latch. By Yamac Eren Ay.</figcaption></figure><p>On top of SR-Latch, one can create an even more sophisticated circuit called D-Latch, which can be regarded as a wrapper for the two most basic memory operations, namely <em>saving</em> and <em>reading</em> data. So, all these binary programs lay the groundwork for <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random-access_memory">Random Access Memory</a> (RAM) and make the computers <em>remember</em> something.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*2nxyaK-eIpCBE0aqpuygqg.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/320/1*eC4UvAOpBHd2WyauuSlYVQ.jpeg" /><figcaption>Left: D-Latch, Right: RAM (with D-Latch as unit.)</figcaption></figure><p>Remind yourself that this is just a logical implementation of memory, and possibly, one of the endless many. Such models are <em>useful</em> for understanding how memory in our brain works, but NOT sufficient. For this, let’s consider how the information might be stored and evaluated in our brain, without going much into neuroscientific details.</p><h4>Memory in Neural Circuits</h4><p>In my previous article series (linked below), I briefly mentioned how the neural spike data can be learned to use non-parameter density estimation, and briefly explained that neural spikes correspond to <em>1s</em> and the rest to <em>0s.</em> In fact, I meant temporal coding.</p><ul><li><a href="https://yamaceay.medium.com/probability-distributions-1-8eddc5e434e9">Probability Distributions 1</a></li><li><a href="https://yamaceay.medium.com/probability-distributions-3-cb12030287e9">Probability Distributions 3</a></li></ul><p>However, it is challenging to match two spike samples due to the apparent randomness and <em>sparsity</em> of the process. It might not be economically viable for the brain to encode all information with such fine granularity and precision.</p><p>A more plausible approach would be to encode information using the expected instantaneous spike rate <em>⟨r⟩</em>, the temporal change of the spike rate on average. Below are two simple examples of how to sample such instantaneous spike rate distributions:</p><ol><li><em>Wire together, fire together</em>: Simulating similar neurons firing at the same time.</li><li><em>History repeats itself</em>: Simulating a neuron at different time points.</li></ol><p>Rate coding is generally more relevant because it allows our brain to learn <em>stable</em> neural states called “attractors”. In the plot below, read the state as the spike rate and the energy as the probability of transitioning to another state. It is the hardest to switch to another state, if the state is stuck in one of those two attractors. So, this is how a neuron corresponds to a D-Latch encoding binary states.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/378/1*vJ2sLqBr4E-LtWnFzz2YgQ.png" /><figcaption>Visualization of attractors. <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopfield_network">Source.</a></figcaption></figure><p>Technically, if you put many binary neurons together to form a fully connected neural network, you can achieve a similar behavior as RAM.</p><p>Enter <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopfield_network">Hopfield Networks</a>: A <em>recurrent</em> neural network consisting of a list of neurons which are connected to each other, where the strength of the connection between two neurons are given by the pairwise weights <em>w{ij}</em>.</p><p>Given that the high states are encoded as <em>+1</em> and low states as <em>-1</em> (and not <em>0</em>), the energy function <em>E</em> of the Hopfield network looks like:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/136/1*CJpzztBJT2pMsNwhWHyZsA.png" /><figcaption>Energy function <em>E</em>.</figcaption></figure><p>Using this function, we can define stable states (attractors) of the network. The system evolves by updating the state of each neuron to minimize the energy. If two neurons share the same state and have a strong connection, the energy is at its lowest. Do you now understand what “wire together, fire together” means?</p><p>The update rule (recurrence relation) for the state of neuron <em>i</em> is given by:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/210/1*xR5K2V49bY3UwaDHAZKnpw.png" /><figcaption>Recurrent update function s.</figcaption></figure><p>where <em>sign(x)</em> is the sign function that returns <em>+1</em> if <em>x</em> is positive and <em>-1</em> if <em>x</em> is negative. By iteratively applying this update rule, the network can recall stored patterns from partial / noisy inputs. So, associative short-term memory in its purest form!</p><h4>Memory in Reinforcement Learning</h4><figure><img alt="https://towardsdatascience.com/crystal-clear-reinforcement-learning-7e6c1541365e" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*A_VajGCcbT-_YiHaNXbQOw.png" /><figcaption>A Reinforcement Learning environment. <a href="https://towardsdatascience.com/crystal-clear-reinforcement-learning-7e6c1541365e">Source.</a></figcaption></figure><p>Now, we proceed to a completely different field: <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforcement_learning">Reinforcement Learning</a>! In simple words, an agent (e.g. a person) observes the current state (e.g. belly fat), performs an action (e.g. working out) with the intention to maximize its future rewards (e.g. healthy life) and transitions to the next state (e.g. less belly fat).</p><p>In theory, there are cases (totally qualified as <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chain">Markov chains</a>) where an agent can <em>fully</em> observe its exact state, where memory is not necessary. Take chess for example: If you are given any game position, you already have all the information you need to make the most well-informed move, and you can simply <em>forget</em> what exactly happened at the game opening.</p><figure><img alt="https://www.scaler.com/topics/artificial-intelligence-tutorial/pomdp/" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/868/1*-RCwtn9XcbuDjWfazOJWIw.png" /><figcaption>Partially observable Markov decision processes. <a href="https://www.scaler.com/topics/artificial-intelligence-tutorial/pomdp/">Source.</a></figcaption></figure><p>However, in practice, it is not possible to entirely observe states but only a small subset of features roughly describing the current state, and the quality of such observations affect directly how the agent <em>perceives</em> its environment. In the same chess example, if you can only observe the last move (and not the whole board), then you definitely need all previous observations to be able to provide a full picture of the chess board. So, can we suggest that retaining the entire history of observations yields the best outcomes?</p><figure><img alt="https://lod2.eu/stack/" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/214/1*LpLZWQGjFCpbrsr7vf_nZw.jpeg" /><figcaption>Visualization of a database. <a href="https://lod2.eu/stack/">Source.</a></figcaption></figure><p>Accuracy-wise, yes, but it introduces a huge performance drawback. See that this requires significant memory resources, and gets computationally very expensive, especially if the number of observations grows very fast. A solution proposal would be to just accept the fate of the trade-off and store a <em>limited</em> number of previous observations instead of the full history. Can we do better?</p><p>A possibly more practical approach to persist the previous states into the memory is to maintain a belief system, which I tried to explain in the article below:</p><p><a href="https://yamaceay.medium.com/probability-distributions-2-bac9bbed470f">Probability Distributions 2</a></p><p>The key takeaway from my article: More than often, it suffices to learn a few <em>characteristics</em> to be able to make well-informed decisions, and not all states. Same as trying to find the average weight of a group of people after someone joins to the group. Instead of summing all weights and dividing by the number of people each time someone joins, you can just use the previous average value to calculate it on the fly.</p><figure><img alt="https://www.quora.com/Is-a-single-layered-ReLu-network-still-a-universal-approximator" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/225/1*7vyUk3Ud-eZgbD4TNxt0PQ.png" /><figcaption>Fitting a curve at an arbitrary precision. <a href="https://www.quora.com/Is-a-single-layered-ReLu-network-still-a-universal-approximator">Source.</a></figcaption></figure><p>However, remind yourself that some models might be useful, but all models are <em>wrong</em>. A naive belief system lacks the ability to generalize well to real-life use cases, for example, the weight distribution of a group of people must not necessarily be normally distributed. This is a drawback of naive belief systems where prior assumptions are made about the underlying ground-truth, so we need a more <em>data-driven</em><strong> </strong>approach. From now on, keep in mind that the memory won’t remain exactly the “same” anymore (recall from above).</p><h4>Memory in Neural Networks</h4><p>The challenge now is how to estimate an arbitrary distribution in the most general way possible. Enter Deep Learning models, universal function approximators. I’ve explained this concept in simple terms in another article of mine, so feel free to check it out:</p><p><a href="https://pub.aimind.so/transfer-learning-for-dummies-a-gentle-introduction-b47df8f1cd92">Transfer Learning For Dummies: A Gentle Introduction</a></p><p>It’s crucial to recognize that Deep Learning is not merely a single algorithm or model architecture. Rather, it represents the most comprehensive <em>framework</em> for all ML models, and incorporates a wide variety of architectures, best practices, challenges and learning components, see below for example:</p><figure><img alt="https://medium.com/r/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.labellerr.com%2Fblog%2Fall-about-deep-learning-models-that-you-should-know%2F" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/850/1*cpgG7xhdAwzMmcnxGtG6hg.png" /><figcaption>Different (artificial) neural network architectures. <a href="https://www.labellerr.com/blog/all-about-deep-learning-models-that-you-should-know/">Source.</a></figcaption></figure><p>Typically, in a classical problem, the model is expected to return an answer based on the input without considering the previous answers. However, in tasks, where the sequence of inputs is key, such as Natural Language Processing (NLP), we need a way to remember previous information, or as mentioned often enough in this article, <em>recurrence</em> in neural networks! Enter Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), as shown in (e).</p><p>Luckily, I came across an article in geeksforgeeks.org which covers them very well, absolutely recommended:</p><p><a href="https://www.geeksforgeeks.org/introduction-to-recurrent-neural-network/">Introduction to Recurrent Neural Networks - GeeksforGeeks</a></p><p>Despite the fancy wording, RNNs are actually quite straightforward: they are ML models that, in addition to taking the standard input <em>x{t}</em>, also consider the previous output <em>h{t-1}</em> when predicting the current output <em>h{t}</em>. This allows the model to learn and maintain an <em>internal</em> state <em>h</em>, which effectively represents its belief based on incoming updates <em>x</em>. Unlike traditional belief systems that rely on strict assumptions, this internal state is an approximate, data-driven representation.</p><figure><img alt="https://medium.com/analytics-vidhya/natural-language-processing-from-basics-to-using-rnn-and-lstm-ef6779e4ae66" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*7j6l3Ha5rPLZuBBdDJjlSg.png" /><figcaption>A simple generative task performed by RNNs. <a href="https://medium.com/analytics-vidhya/natural-language-processing-from-basics-to-using-rnn-and-lstm-ef6779e4ae66">Source.</a></figcaption></figure><p>In theory, this enables RNNs to achieve short-term memory in its most fundamental form. However, in practice, RNNs can struggle with retaining information from far earlier in the sequence due to a problem known as vanishing gradients. This issue causes the influence of earlier data points to diminish with the increasing time distance, which leads to difficulties in learning <em>long-term dependencies</em>.</p><figure><img alt="https://medium.com/r/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.linkedin.com%2Fpulse%2Frnn-lstm-gru-why-do-we-need-them-suvankar-maity-joegc" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*507-5uCAOKGwu6eBujbUsA.png" /><figcaption>Comparison of different sequential model architectures. Left: RNN, Middle: LSTM, Right: GRU. <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/rnn-lstm-gru-why-do-we-need-them-suvankar-maity-joegc">Source.</a></figcaption></figure><p>To address this, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) were developed. These are advanced types of RNNs designed to remember long-term information more effectively. LSTMs introduce a mechanism to better control the <em>flow of information</em>, allowing them to maintain and retrieve relevant information over longer sequences. It has been experimentally shown that LSTMs can remember up to 1000 previous states, making them significantly more powerful than standard RNNs for tasks requiring long-term memory.</p><p>However, LSTMs still treat all pieces of information with <em>equal</em> importance, which can be inefficient and quite noisy. This limitation is tackled by Transformer models, which introduce a mechanism called Self-Attention. Self-Attention dynamically weights the importance of previous states depending on their relevance to the task at hand.</p><figure><img alt="https://medium.com/carbon-consulting/transformer-architecture-how-transformer-models-work-46fc70b4ea59" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/560/1*VSn4mLzsjoQyVTIX-qylXg.png" /><figcaption>Encoder-decoder transformer architecture. <a href="https://medium.com/carbon-consulting/transformer-architecture-how-transformer-models-work-46fc70b4ea59">Source.</a></figcaption></figure><p>This means that the model can prioritize key information from the past, similar to how human memory works. Humans do not process all information equally, but rather focus on the most relevant details in a noisy environment. Transformers mimic this kind of <em>selective attention</em>, which makes them way more powerful than their predecessors.</p><figure><img alt="https://research.google/blog/transformer-a-novel-neural-network-architecture-for-language-understanding/" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*jeQjHuAMq13443kAfKsMwg.png" /><figcaption>Selective attention achieved by transformers while performing text generation. <a href="https://research.google/blog/transformer-a-novel-neural-network-architecture-for-language-understanding/">Source.</a></figcaption></figure><p>From this point, you’ll encounter a wide variety of algorithms and countless hybrid or novel approaches, so don’t be scared by the quite complex landscape, and see the bigger frame. There are numerous articles that explain the transformer architecture in detail, I recommend some articles that you might find interesting:</p><ul><li><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762">Original paper presenting Attention Mechanism</a>. It is one of the golden articles of our century, without any doubt.</li><li><a href="https://towardsdatascience.com/transformers-141e32e69591">An article published in Towards Data Science</a>, which explains the Transformer architecture but also tells the motivation behind Transformers way better than I did. Keep up the good work!</li><li><a href="https://www.turing.com/kb/brief-introduction-to-transformers-and-their-power">Another article that thoroughly covers everything about Transformers</a>.</li><li><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.08621">Retentive Networks, regarded as the successors of Transformers</a>. After it was published, I nearly lost my interest in Transformer networks.</li></ul><h4>Memory in Large Language Models</h4><p>As a bonus topic, I wanted to briefly point out the currently most researched form of memory: <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model">Large Language Models (LLMs)</a>. Nowadays, we see large language models (LLMs) everywhere, from language translation to content creation and chat programs. Take <a href="https://openai.com/chatgpt/">ChatGPT</a> for example, a chat program from OpenAI which has become an integral part of our daily lives, thanks to its accessibility and high performance.</p><figure><img alt="https://www.gruender.de/kuenstliche-intelligenz/gpt-4-kostenlos-nutzen-239152/" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*RrnzxG6sroueK4NR19TLzQ.jpeg" /><figcaption>GPT-4 from OpenAI. <a href="https://www.gruender.de/kuenstliche-intelligenz/gpt-4-kostenlos-nutzen-239152/">Source.</a></figcaption></figure><p>It’s built on top of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_pre-trained_transformer">GPT ( = <strong>G</strong>enerative <strong>P</strong>re-trained <strong>T</strong>ransformer)</a> models with hundreds of billions (and even trillions) of learned parameters, trained on high-quality data such as Wikipedia articles. This huge amount of parametric information allows the model to understand context and determine which tokens in a sequence to focus on. Basically, the model has already learnt what and how to “memorize”, which makes it the perfect candidate for a chatbot.</p><p>ChatGPT is just a fancy wrapper for these sequential models, and nothing new. A very cheesy but concrete example: just open a fresh conversation, and type a few basic information about yourself, e.g. your age is <em>21</em>. Even after a 50 message long conversation about music tastes (which have nothing to do with your age), if you ask the bot again how old you are, the bot will most probably remember it correctly, as you can see below. Seemingly the closest to how we memorize and remember in real-life!</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1024/1*_kWDHLJ241wbPjcORFL2MQ.png" /><figcaption>ChatGPT conversation. By Yamac Eren Ay</figcaption></figure><p>Finally, I don’t want it to sound like I’m solely advertising for the most modern approaches. We stand on the shoulders of giants, and it’s often easier to build on top of a system than to create one from scratch. ChatGPT is fancy indeed, but not as fancy as its predecessors.</p><h4>Final Thoughts</h4><p>As we’ve explored throughout this series, memory is a fundamental aspect of both biological and artificial systems, and it seems to be more complicated than just “remembering”. From the basic mechanisms in electric circuits to the sophisticated models in neural networks, understanding how memory works can provide deep insights into the nature of intelligence and learning.</p><p>To keep this article as simple as possible, I left the biological aspects untouched, but if you’re interested, I recommend you to check this out, which answers how the brain stores memories:</p><p><a href="https://qbi.uq.edu.au/brain-basics/memory/where-are-memories-stored">Where are memories stored in the brain?</a></p><p>Whether you’re a student, a researcher, or simply an enthusiast, I hope this series has provided you with valuable insights and sparked your curiosity to further explore the fascinating world of memory in various domains. I’m happy to receive your feedback!</p><hr><p><a href="https://thequantasticjournal.com/weirdest-explanation-of-memory-727147f14ac9">Memory In Various Contexts</a> was originally published in <a href="https://thequantasticjournal.com">The Quantastic Journal</a> on Medium, where people are continuing the conversation by highlighting and responding to this story.</p>
