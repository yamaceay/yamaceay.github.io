<h4>Why and how to simplify the way we prove something?</h4><p>We live in a world with bunch of sophistication, over-engineering and information overload, where we are tempted to use buzzwords and virtual realities to fill the void of lack of basic understanding.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*qhquvmRyLqao72nASmg-Ag.jpeg" /><figcaption>Source: Life Is Complicated, and That Is Good, aier.org by Stephen Davies</figcaption></figure><p>In general, the computer science industry is filled with developers who seem to advertise their smarter way of solving the problems, and drift away day-by-day from the absolute simplicity.</p><p>Take logical and mathematical proofs for example: There might be lots of ways to solve a simple problem such as calculating the area of a rectangle, yet only a few manage to be elegant and simple at the same time.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*MnUhc6GgUli6ih5HF1K-5w.jpeg" /><figcaption>Source: Geometry — 24 — Proofs Using Definition, youtube.com by by thenewboston</figcaption></figure><p>Proof systems, for example proving that the the area of rectangle is the product of the width and the height of the rectangle, come in various shapes and sizes. Each proof system comes with its own set of derivation rules, which are used to prove theorems by constructing proofs.</p><p>A thumb rule says that the more sophisticated the rules of a proof system are, the more special cases and branches it has, and the more difficult it is to make a completeness proof. So, this leads to the quest for minimal proof systems with as few simple rules as possible.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*A7N6c8lUa7dpMVzRHax7TA.png" /><figcaption>Source: Reduction (Complexity), wikipedia.org</figcaption></figure><p>Take Turing-completeness for example: In theory, one can represent all programs and functions using a hypothetical Turing machine, which reads and writes on a tape with ternary symbols “True”,“False” and “Null” (or “#”), one step at at time. So, one can simply reduce the existence of a solution to a complex problem to the existence of an equivalent Turing machine, without having to reinvent the wheel.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*BDs8D_Jtb7yKMAK86mKADg.gif" /><figcaption>Source: A Turing Machine simulator written in Pharo, medium.com by Julien Delplanque</figcaption></figure><p>Likewise, it’s also possible to reduce all such algorithms to general µ-recursive functions with arbitrarily many arguments, as proposed by Gödel. In this specific setting, the equivalence class of all computable functions is now called While-completeness.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*0H2ej3gSDAnuk7FWKO4Gjw.png" /><figcaption>Source: Primitive recursive function, wikipedia.org</figcaption></figure><p>Here’s the problem: If the ultimate goal is to write human-readable proofs while reducing manual errors and avoiding repetitive work, Turing-completeness or While-completeness proofs are still way too difficult to work with (for me personally, but you can keep wrestling with Turing machines).</p><p>Now, enter Lambda Calculus (invented by Church), another great modeling language to describe or prove a theorem, aside from Turing machines and Gödel’s µ-recursive functions. In fact, it proves to be much practical, intuitive and useful, to name a few: inspiring the birth of category theory, type systems, and functional programming, and aligning with mathematics, logic and natural sciences.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*q7vU-4WnwoT4PEAaOqxlWw.png" /><figcaption>Source: Category Theory, wikipedia.org</figcaption></figure><p>In its simplest form, (untyped) Lambda Calculus is a Turing-complete language, which boils down into three main building blocks. (1) A variable <strong>x</strong>, is a symbol representing real values such as <strong>“hello”</strong> or <strong>2</strong>. (2) An abstraction of <strong>x</strong> over a term <strong>M</strong>, written as <strong>λx.M</strong>, represents a function <strong>x ↦ M(x)</strong>, mapping <strong>x</strong> to <strong>M(x)</strong>. (3) An application of <strong>y</strong> to an abstraction <strong>λx.M</strong>, written as <strong>(λx.M)y</strong>, represents a function call, which must evaluate to <strong>M[x := y]</strong>, also the substitution of <strong>x</strong> for <strong>y</strong> in term <strong>M</strong>.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*kC18QDA9Y0jOv2zqFAN4tw.png" /><figcaption>Source: bach.ai, The Lambda Calculus for Absolute Dummies by Joscha Bach</figcaption></figure><p>In Lambda Calculus, functions are regarded as first-class citizens, also a function can take other functions as arguments, and return another function. With this in mind, functions with multiple arguments <strong>f(x, y, z)</strong> can be “Curried”, also declared and called as partial functions: Given a function <strong>f = λx . λy . λz . M</strong>, <strong>f x</strong> returns <strong>λy . λz . M</strong>, <strong>f x y</strong> returns <strong>λz . M</strong> and <strong>f x y z</strong> returns <strong>M</strong>.</p><p>Furthermore, a function <strong>f : A → B</strong> is formally defined as a left-total, right-unique relation, given two sets <strong>A</strong> and <strong>B</strong>. In other words, for each input <strong>x ∈ A</strong>, there must be exactly one output <strong>f(x) ∈ B</strong>. Therefore, a function call <strong>f(3, 6, 8)</strong> must return the same result, if terminated, regardless of the calculation order of the underlying partial functions (e.g. <strong>f(x, 6, 8)3</strong> and <strong>f(3, 6, z)8</strong> must be equal). Using that information, one can define an equivalence class of terms with the same final outcomes, called <strong>β</strong>-evaluation (Beta-evaluation).</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*pt-uSPAThi3_bLVQNa1QNQ.png" /><figcaption>Source: Confluence (abstract rewriting), wikipedia.org</figcaption></figure><p>In the untyped setting, there are some absurd loopholes which makes the proofs theoretically impossible. For example, the self-application is legal, also that an abstraction term takes itself as an argument, e.g. <strong>(λx.x)(λx.x)</strong>, although it doesn’t seem right. A more extreme one: the calculation of a term may not terminate at all, e.g. <strong>(λx.xx)(λx.xx)</strong>, which is a relaxation which would make proving / disproving impossible in general. These drawbacks of untypedness lead to the necessity of typed Lambda Calculus.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*sjj5Tkbdm_ux4kqQ323oGA.png" /><figcaption>Source: The Turing Machine Halting Problem, medium.com by Brent Morgan</figcaption></figure><p>The single extra assumption needed in typed Lambda Calculus is that each legal term <strong>x</strong> must be of one unique type <strong>σ</strong>, written as <strong>x : σ</strong>. Given <strong>M : 𝜏</strong>, an abstraction <strong>λx : σ.M</strong> is of type σ → 𝜏, and given further that <strong>y</strong> is of valid input type <strong>σ</strong>, an application <strong>(λx : σ.M)y</strong> is of type <strong>𝜏</strong>. This implies that the self-application is no longer allowed, since a function <strong>f: σ → 𝜏</strong> only takes input of type <strong>σ</strong> and not of type <strong>σ → 𝜏</strong>. Followingly, all terms of a Beta equivalence class can be calculated in finite steps and must have the same type.</p><h4>Bye-Bye Self-Application (For Now), Typed Lambda Calculus Is Here!</h4><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*DQqP0skBn0pXuthc0uzTFQ.png" /><figcaption>Source: What is Type Safe? stackoverflow.com</figcaption></figure><p>Before delving deep into other Lambda Calculi, it is interesting to note another concept. A variable <strong>x</strong> if free in a term <strong>M</strong>, if the value of <strong>x</strong> is picked independent of <strong>M</strong>. Followingly, a variable <strong>x</strong> is bound in a term <strong>λx : σ.M</strong>, since the value of <strong>x</strong> in <strong>M</strong> is manipulated by the binding variable as in <strong>λx : σ</strong>. As shown, it is possible to make a term free of free-variable, using the binding variables on top. Instead, those free occurrences can be bounded in a context of declarations <strong>Γ</strong>. So the type declaration <strong>λx : σ.M : σ → 𝜏 </strong>would be equivalent to the following judgment: given a context <strong>Γ = x : σ</strong>, the term <strong>M</strong> is of type <strong>𝜏</strong>. In general, a judgment looks like: <strong>Γ ⊢ A : B</strong>, given a context <strong>Γ</strong> and a declaration <strong>A : B</strong>.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*_Qrc6GisEE4gO5dhaukGdw.png" /><figcaption>Source: Lambda Cube, wikipedia.org</figcaption></figure><p>The simply typed Lambda Calculus can be extended in different directions, namely:</p><ul><li>Lambda Polymorphic (with terms having generic types). For example, the polymorphic identity function <strong>λσ : ☆. λx : σ . x</strong> is of type <strong>∏σ : ☆. σ → σ</strong>.</li><li>Lambda Sub-omega (with type constructors taking types as input). For example, the identity type constructor <strong>λσ : ☆. σ</strong> is of type <strong>☆ → ☆</strong>.</li><li>Lambda Predicate (with type constructors taking terms as input). For example, the predicate <strong>∏x : σ . P x </strong>with <strong>σ : ☆</strong> and <strong>P : σ → ☆ </strong>is of type <strong>σ → ☆</strong>.</li></ul><p>The binary combination of all those term — type dependencies leads to the emergence of various Lambda calculi, and most importantly the Lambda Cube (<strong>λC</strong>, the Calculus of Constructions), which allows all kinds of dependence between terms and types.</p><h4>The Lambda Cube Sounds Nice, Let’s Just Stick With It!</h4><p>By the way, ever heard of Curry-Howard correspondence? It is an intuitive approach to visualize types as theorems and terms of such types as proofs of theorems respectively. Let’s say we want to prove that “<em>There is a prime number greater than 15</em>”, which corresponds to the type “<em>Prime numbers which are greater than 15</em>”. Since we know that the term <strong>17</strong> is an inhabitant of this type, the theorem is proven by <strong>17</strong>.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*txF-eIsV3rag6z4dJLWxEw.png" /><figcaption>Source: What order logic does a system correspond to under Curry–Howard correspondence?, cs.stackexchange.com</figcaption></figure><p>Followingly, the Constructive Logic can be simulated by <strong>λC</strong> using some simple tricks:</p><ul><li>The implication can be defined trivially as <strong>⇒(A, B) := A → B</strong>. If this type has an inhabitant, let’s say a function <strong>f : A → B</strong>, then the existence of a term <strong>x : A</strong> guarantees that <strong>f x : B</strong>. In other words, the proof of <strong>⇒(A, B)</strong> proves that <strong>A</strong> implies <strong>B</strong>. It can simply be defined in all calculi with terms dependent on terms.</li><li>Enter absurdity <strong>⊥ := ∏α : ☆ . α</strong>, whose inhabitants, if exist, could return a term of type <strong>α</strong>, if given type <strong>α</strong>. In other words, the proof of absurdity <strong>⊥</strong> would prove every theorem <strong>α</strong>, which is absurd. Using that, one can define <strong>¬(A) := A → ⊥</strong>, which evaluates to true (being provable), only if the given theorem <strong>A</strong>. These can be expressed in calculi where the terms can depend on types.</li><li>And there is conjunction <strong>∧(A, B) := ∏C : ☆ . (A → B → C) → C</strong>, whose inhabitants, if exist, is sufficient for building a term of type <strong>A</strong> and a term of type <strong>B</strong>. In other words, the proof of conjunction <strong>∧(A, B)</strong> would prove <strong>A</strong> and <strong>B</strong>. Similarly, one can define disjunction as <strong>∨(A, B) := ∏C : ☆ . (A → C) → (B → C) → C</strong>, which would be proven, if <strong>A</strong> or <strong>B</strong> were proven. These can only be expressed in calculi with types depending on types. If, additionally, the terms depending on types were allowed, we could define equivalent conjunction and disjunction type constructors, namely <strong>∧2(A, B) := ¬(A → ¬B) </strong>and <strong>∨2(A, B) := ¬A → B</strong>.</li><li>Take universal quantification <strong>∀(S, P) := ∏x : S . P x</strong> for a final example, given a set <strong>S : ☆</strong> and a predicate <strong>P : S → ☆</strong>, mapping each item in <strong>S</strong> to a theorem. The inhabitant of this type would be a function mapping the occurrences <strong>x : S</strong> to the proofs <strong>u : P x</strong>. To give an example, <strong>S</strong> is the set of all positive integers and <strong>P</strong> is an unary predicate stating “<em>&lt;x&gt; is greater than zero</em>”. In a similar fashion, the existential quantification <strong>∃(S, P) := ∏α : ☆ . (∏x : S . P x → α) → α</strong> can be defined. These expressions are allowed in calculi with types depending on terms. If, additionally, terms depending on types are allowed, we can construct an alternative existential quantifier <strong>∃2(S, P) := ¬ ∏x : S . ¬ P x</strong>.</li></ul><p>Another important side-note is that the derivation rules for <strong>λC</strong> are used to construct and evaluate proofs in the type theory. These rules specify how types relate to each other within a context <strong>Γ</strong>, guiding the formation of valid expressions and their transformations. Here’s a concise overview of some key derivation rules in <strong>λC</strong>:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*f_oZNZZRjXoziQFrKR5ozA.png" /><figcaption>Source: my lecture notes</figcaption></figure><p>For the sake of mentioning, let’s explain briefly which rule is suitable for what use:</p><ul><li><em>sort</em>: Establishes the base types and their hierarchy.</li><li><em>var</em>: Introduces a new variable of a certain type into the context.</li><li><em>weak</em>: Allows the extension of the context with new assumptions without affecting existing derivations.</li><li><em>form</em>: Defines the formation rules for function types, both dependent (<strong>Π</strong>) and non-dependent (<strong>→</strong>).</li><li><em>appl</em>: Describes how function applications are typed, taking into account both dependent and non-dependent functions.</li><li><em>abst</em>: Gives the rules for abstracting expressions into lambda functions, again considering both dependent and non-dependent types.</li><li><em>conv</em>: States that if two types are equivalent (via <strong>β</strong>-reduction), an expression of one type can be considered to have the other type.</li></ul><p>Using the Constructive Logic, one can theoretically express any logical formula, but cannot prove all valid formulas without further modification. For example, using vanilla <strong>λC</strong>, one can prove that <strong>¬ ¬ ¬ α → ¬ α</strong> is inhabited, but not <strong>¬ ¬ α → α</strong>. To unleash the full potential of <strong>λC</strong> in theorem evaluation, we have to add at least one of the two missing logical axioms as specified below:</p><ul><li><em>DN</em>: Double-negation law, encoded as the type <strong>∏α : ☆ . ¬ ¬ α → α</strong>.</li><li><em>ET</em>: Excluded-third law, encoded as the type <strong>∏α : ☆ . α ∨ ¬ α</strong>.</li></ul><p>Adding a proof of <em>DN</em> or <em>ET </em>into the context <strong>Γ</strong> allows us to evaluate all logical formulas entirely.</p><h4>My Doctor Prescribed Me Dynamic Programming and Definitions For Less Headaches</h4><p>There is another problem on sight. A complete derivation proof — without necessary improvements — might be a bit problematic: Overlapping subterms, subtypes etc. could increase the number of derivation lines dramatically. In order to make the proofs shorter, we can rely on dynamic programming and eliminate the duplicate lines without changing the derivation, akin to hashmapping each derivation step to get a unique representation.</p><p>Additionally, skipping some trivial derivation steps might save even more time, such as skipping the type validation step while analyzing function <strong>f : A → A → A</strong>.</p><p>Assuming that a derivation step is shared across many different proofs, steps of the base proof are repeated exactly as many times as the number of its successor proofs. To refactor the proofs (as in a computer program), enter the definitions, which can be regarded as proof abstraction across derivations. An illustration:</p><ul><li>Define <strong>a(x, y) := M : N</strong></li><li>Reuse<strong> a(z, z) </strong>in <strong><em>proof1</em> := λf : N → N . λz : A . f a(z, z) : N</strong></li><li>Reuse <strong>a(y, z)</strong> in <strong><em>proof2</em> := λy : A . λz : A . λg : N → M. g a(y, z) : M</strong></li></ul><p>In the new settings, each line of a derivation step contains a definition with a unique identifier name, and the number of definitions increase one-by-one. This means, overwriting or undoing the definitions is not allowed, which would be useful for removing unnecessary local term definitions. This problem can be fixed by introducing definition scopes called environments, also the list of previous definitions at a derivation step. By means of adding the necessary derivation rules to integrate the definitions into the <strong>λC</strong>, we get <strong>λD</strong>, the Lambda Definition as follows:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*NAKlbiVACtrJ6QcQ23_c3g.png" /><figcaption>Source: my lecture notes</figcaption></figure><p>With this in mind, <strong>λD</strong> provides a framework for defining all logical and mathematical objects, expressions and their relationships in a more refactored, practical manner. Using that, one can define terms / types corresponding to real-life objects, for example: natural numbers, basic numeric operations, sets, relations, groups.</p><p>If you feel interested, there are two more matters that I want to quickly mention before delving into the final discussion: Firstly, the Church numerals as a way of representing natural numbers. For a real-life implementation, you can check my previous article:</p><p><a href="https://yamaceay.medium.com/learn-with-me-reasonml-part-2-6f704e0ded0b">Learn With Me: ReasonML, Part 2</a></p><p>Before introducing the second bonus, let’s go back to the untyped Lambda Calculus, which can be reduced further step-by-step to a more minimal language. Define the following terms (which have to remain untyped for allowing self-application):</p><ul><li><strong>I := λx . x</strong></li><li><strong>K := λxy . x</strong></li><li><strong>S := λxyz . x z (y z)</strong></li><li><strong>ι := λf . f S K</strong></li></ul><p>Using solely <strong>I</strong>, <strong>K</strong> and <strong>S</strong>, one can define all terms in untyped Lambda Calculus (see [1] for more details). Interestingly, <strong>I</strong> can be reduced to <strong>K</strong> and <strong>S</strong> as well, since <strong>I := S K K</strong>. Another surprising fact: <strong>ι</strong> would suffice to express both <strong>K := ι (ι (ι ι))</strong> and <strong>S := ι (ι (ι (ι ι)))</strong>, making it possible to define all terms using a single term.</p><h4>Final Notes on The Drawbacks of Minimalism and Their Real-Life Implications</h4><p>All in all, the perfectly minimal proof systems are the most desirable way to make completeness proofs without lacking the soundness, but in theory. In fact, this would introduce a huge hierarchy of rules, immediately leading to performance drawbacks and unnecessary overhead due to rules being stacked on top of each other.</p><p>For example, adding <strong>Σ</strong>-dependent types (sum types) to a proof system could possibly reduce the number of calculation steps while also increasing the number of proof cases. In theory, the already existing calculus is complete with either product types (<strong>∏</strong>) or sum types (<strong>Σ</strong>); so there would be no need to integrate <strong>Σ</strong> too into proofs. In practice, it would cost much less calculation steps to analyze an existential quantifier defined as <strong>Σx : S . P x</strong> (compared to <strong>∃(S, P) := ∏α : ☆ . (∏x : S . P x → α) → α</strong>).</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*pWp_61X70yf6iH5hjGjfDA.jpeg" /><figcaption>Source: Trade-off Theory, wallstreetprep.com</figcaption></figure><p>Ever heard of Hierarchy vs. Market Trade-off? Let’s say that you are the founder of a new start-up. On the one hand, outsourcing a reasonably expensive service of a well-established company (or using their advertisement resources), could help develop and launch your product faster and more reliably. On the other hand, self-advertisement and building the brand from scratch would mean more market and less hierarchical costs. The goal is to find the optimal balance between hierarchical and market costs, as in other trade-offs that I explained earlier in the article below:</p><p><a href="https://medium.com/thedevproject/how-understanding-tradeoffs-made-me-realize-99f2747052b8">How Understanding Tradeoffs Made Me Realize …</a></p><p><strong>Same idea, different settings</strong>: If you have read my other article explaining the hierarchical approach of NN modeling, you may have seen the experiment illustrated at the end of my article. In that concrete case, there was an unusual increase in losses when stacking pre-trained models (AND, OR, NOT) to train a more complex XOR model. Instead, I could just train a XOR model from scratch.</p><p><a href="https://pub.aimind.so/transfer-learning-for-dummies-a-gentle-introduction-b47df8f1cd92">Transfer Learning For Dummies: A Gentle Introduction</a></p><p>With this in mind, such extremely minimal proof systems are — in my personal opinion — mostly suitable for pure intellectual entertainment and formal proofs, but not ideally for real-life use cases, where we rather overcomplicate the algorithms and add special use cases and shortcuts to increase the program speed and efficiency. So, this marks the end of my article.
