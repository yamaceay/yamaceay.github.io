<p>Why, how and when to transfer the knowledge across different neural networks?</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*wgLRvECi1cf-MGuUrtlZHA.png" /><figcaption>Figure 1: Knowledge Transfer</figcaption></figure><h4>The Power of Knowledge Transfer</h4><p>In the ever-evolving landscape of business and technology, the concept of knowledge transfer plays a key role, and it is much more closely related to software engineering and machine learning than you think.</p><p>It's <em>obvious</em> that we cannot master every technical detail ourselves. If you have a business idea, you can drive yourself to insanity by delving deep into the <strong>rabbit hole</strong> and trying to learn every technical aspect from scratch.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:2216/1*RIbrtLoXyhaf6TRyjYLh6A.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:2964/1*ZxsssyEgIGjw-132QvlRGA.png" /><figcaption>Figure 2 (Left): Map of Computer Science, Figure 3 (Right): 7 Layers of Internet</figcaption></figure><p>For example, the whole study field of software engineering revolves around abstraction:</p><ul><li><strong>Bits</strong> — in the form of transistors and wires — form the basis for the most fundamental arithmetic and logical operations (ALU) and storing binary information in artificial memory (RAM), leading to the invention of the CPU.</li><li>CPU <strong>instructions</strong> constitute the simplest computer programs, which are built upon each other to create operating systems (OS) and high-level programming languages such as C, Python, Julia, and others.</li><li>Beyond <strong>programming languages</strong>, coding environments and tools are utilized to develop high-level applications, such as text-based machine learning models like <a href="https://openai.com/blog/chatgpt">ChatGPT</a> or coding assistants like <a href="https://github.com/features/copilot">GitHub Copilot</a>.</li></ul><p>To avoid the whole process of <strong>reinventing the wheel</strong>, we have to seek expert advice and rely on authorities with technical background. It not only saves time, effort and resources, but also keeps us <em>mentally sane</em>.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*dg_HrdZDHj9tSRTee7ZwiQ.png" /><figcaption>Figure 4: Biological vs. Artificial Neurons</figcaption></figure><h4>From Neurons to Complex Neural Networks</h4><p>In the realm of machine learning, the journey often begins with the most basic learning units, inspired by the human brain's neurons: perceptrons. At their core, perceptrons lay the groundwork for understanding more complex models.</p><p>In simple words: a perceptron takes multiple inputs, calculates a weighted sum of inputs, adds its own bias, then, if needed, passes the result through an activation function to handle nonlinearity.</p><p>Each perceptron can be viewed as a <strong>distinct feature extractor</strong>, and aggregating them into a neural network layer helps extract <strong>more features</strong> and augment the information capacity. This idea leads to single-layer neural networks used for feature extraction.</p><p>Adding more such layers into a more complex model — where the output of a layer is fed onto another layer as input — results in <strong>deeper analysis</strong> of underlying features. This idea leads to hidden layers which are capable of learning the lower-level features.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*C94QImqKD4IMKgId2-Wz0Q.png" /><figcaption>Figure 5: ChemNet as an Example of Large NNs</figcaption></figure><h4>When Neural Networks Are Useful (And When They Are Not)</h4><p>Theoretically, even neural networks with a single hidden layer called Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) are capable of approximating any mathematical function <em>arbitrarily</em>, backed by <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_approximation_theorem">Universal Approximation Theorem</a>. This holds only if the hidden layer is infinitely large.</p><p>More on Multilayer Perceptrons:</p><p><a href="https://towardsdatascience.com/multilayer-perceptron-explained-with-a-real-life-example-and-python-code-sentiment-analysis-cb408ee93141">Multilayer Perceptron Explained with a Real-Life Example and Python Code: Sentiment Analysis</a></p><p>But in the practice, the training of neural networks can be a long, tedious process:</p><ul><li>It often demands <strong>large datasets</strong>, e.g. if you are building an object detection model from scratch, you need millions of images and tons of computational power.</li><li>In terms of <strong>information capacity</strong>, the neural networks should be large, wide and deep enough to be able to store a sufficient amount of information. Consider <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPT-3">GPT3</a>, currently one of the largest language models, which comprises nearly 175 billion parameters.</li><li>Model should sometimes grasp an <strong>entire domain</strong> before addressing a specific task, e.g. if you are building a chatbot, you have to make sure the chatbot learns the English properly before learning the expected dialogues.</li><li>It is necessary to <strong>try different combinations</strong> of hyperparameters and architectural decisions, e.g. training models with 10 different learning rates and 5 different optimizers and picking the best one.</li></ul><p>So, how can we <strong>transfer the knowledge</strong> across different models without going too deep?</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*jKL3EITYEUiJU7qsMLC4pw.png" /><figcaption>Figure 6: Pre-trained vs. Fine-tuned model</figcaption></figure><h4>The Art of Reusing Knowledge from Pre-trained Models</h4><p>Now, let's go back to the main theme with a simple example:</p><ul><li>Our challenge is to train a chatbot B for dealing with customer inquiries for a company.</li><li>Assume that a large language model A is able to generate sentences as perfectly as a native speaker, which has been trained on 50GB+ text data collected from online forums and actual messages of internet users.</li><li>Learning the model B from zero would take up to 30 days — and learning the same model by reusing the model A only as quick as 3 hours — why so different?</li></ul><p>Rather than discarding the valuable knowledge acquired by A, a clever strategy involves <strong>reusing</strong> its capabilities for model B.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*rCQfDmfyrOaaJ-yjtPUCgQ.png" /><figcaption>Figure 7: Illustration of Frozen Weights of Pre-trained Models</figcaption></figure><p>By importing a fraction of model A, more specifically model parameters, into the model B, we can <em>efficiently</em> transfer the model's knowledge to tackle this challenge. Some examples:</p><ul><li>Remove the "head": An image classification model which predicts "whether the person is older than 50", is imported without the final classification layer, and the <strong>underlying high-level features</strong> such as "short black hair" and "brown eyes" are utilized instead.</li><li>Remove the "tail": A language model is imported without the first embedding layer, and integrated in a model where the input data is already embedded.</li></ul><p>The imported model parameters are treated as the <strong>pre-existing knowledge</strong> of a black-box model, which should remain fixed during training. Keep in mind that you cannot interpret why a weight is assigned a value of 2.35 from an external perspective, but you can place <em>trust in someone</em> who knows.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*3nSfdVFoSAs0dngl-3Qp1g.png" /><figcaption>Figure 8: Analogy of Neural Networks being "Blackbox"</figcaption></figure><h4>How to (And How Not to) Choose Pre-trained Models</h4><p>Imagine that each pre-trained model is a domain expert, and a fine-tuned model is a business owner asking domain experts for useful advice.</p><p>The business owner cannot care less about …:</p><ul><li><em>how</em> intelligent and deep is the model? (Model architecture)</li><li><em>how</em> the model makes use of other models? (Model architecture)</li><li><em>how</em> (long) the pre-training took place? (Pre-training)</li></ul><p>She would rather ask …</p><ul><li><em>what</em> does the model require as input? (Input layer)</li><li><em>what</em> is the model expected to return? (Output layer, head)</li><li><em>what</em> are the pros / cons of choosing the model? (Model performance)</li></ul><p>So, she only gets informed about the superficial details about <em>what</em> it is and keeps her sanity by delegating the rest of know-<em>how</em> to the domain experts.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:576/1*7I_eP9UFxk1C0jJ5Ei_0yA.png" /><figcaption>Figure 9: PyTorch</figcaption></figure><p>Now, let's jump into a very simple exercise to demonstrate the effectiveness of <strong>transfer learning</strong> and harness the power of current scientific progress.</p><p>In the following exercise, I will be using <a href="https://pytorch.org/tutorials/beginner/introyt/introyt1_tutorial.html">PyTorch</a>, a powerful yet simple Python library for creating machine learning models in a purely Pythonic fashion.</p><p><strong>Exercise: Learning Logical Gates</strong></p><p>Firstly, let's define a <code>fit()</code> function, which trains a <code>model</code> given features <code>X</code> and labels <code>y</code> for any given number of <code>epochs</code>.</p><p>Given that truth values are assigned either <code>0: False</code> or <code>1: True</code>, the most basic logical operators are: <code>Not(x1)</code>, <code>And(x1, x2)</code>, <code>Or(x1, x2)</code> and <code>Xor(x1, x2)</code> { = <strong><em>either</em></strong> x1 <strong><em>or</em></strong> x2}.</p><p>After training <code>Not</code> and <code>And</code>, we should be expecting the following values:</p><ul><li><code>Not(x1): f(1 — x1) = max(0, 1 — x1)</code></li><li><code>And(x1, x2): f(x1 + x2 — 1) = max(0, x1 + x2 — 1)</code></li></ul><p>where <code>f</code> is ReLU (Rectifying Linear Unit) function, which returns zero, when the number is negative, otherwise the number itself.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1204/1*WRMo3sPb-o3pvXy2TJYhjg.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1356/1*JYPI805XqSciQj76Hx4y2A.png" /><figcaption>Left: NOT(x1), Right: AND(x1,x2)</figcaption></figure><p>Below are the trivial implementations of <code>Not</code> and <code>And</code> as perceptrons (full code: see References below).</p><p>Now, we need a <code>load()</code> function, which imports a pre-trained model, then loads the actual state of learned model parameters into an empty model.</p><p>By integrating <code>Not</code> and <code>And</code> layers into more complex neural networks, we can express <code>Or</code> and <code>Xor</code>:</p><ul><li><code>Or(x1, x2): Not(And(Not(x1), Not(x2)))</code></li><li><code>Xor(x1, x2): And(Or(x1, x2), Or(Not(x1), Not(x2)))</code></li></ul><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1624/1*5ps3Tp-FeDhdzTS42qu0dw.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1608/1*z6zQiKgCKllZB60iV6ChQA.png" /><figcaption>Left: OR(x1,x2), Right: XOR(x1,x2)</figcaption></figure><p>While learning <code>Or</code>: If we don't know what goes into <code>AND1(...)</code>, we can just learn the edges between <code>AND1</code> and inputs <code>x1, x2, ¬x1, ¬x2</code> using a dense layer <code>p</code> with weights of size 4 x 2. This should return the "probability of edge" as output, so let's select sigmoid <code>g(x) = e**x/(e**x + 1)</code> as the activation function, which returns a number between 0 and 1.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:848/1*aCHY16GRpHdINcgCWKqsuw.png" /><figcaption>Trainable Layer Of OR</figcaption></figure><p>Similarly for <code>Xor</code>: Assume we don't know what <code>Or1(...)</code> and <code>Or2(...)</code> should receive as input, and we are given <code>x1, x2</code> and negations <code>¬x1, ¬x2</code>. In order to connect the dots, we are learning another dense layer <code>p</code> with weights of size 4 x 4 and a sigmoid activation function <code>g</code>.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:864/1*T42OcurUs72qhP6LuHBpMQ.png" /><figcaption>Trainable layer of XOR</figcaption></figure><h4>Results &amp; Discussion</h4><p>Let's check the logs:</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1452/1*OV5mqHO2WYTC_PlXuHDMgA.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1620/1*GerKZLDnADA6pbuiftQIBA.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:2148/1*Ksoio-letSHaueiEorI5wQ.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:2488/1*7MsbGkncwKsKWK7mKiCyVQ.png" /><figcaption>Logs of NOT (Upper left), AND (Upper right), OR (Lower left), XOR (Lower right)</figcaption></figure><p>Due to the very simple architecture of the models being discussed here, the results are very likely to get <em>stuck in a local optimum</em> and not improve during training. In such simple exercises, starting the algorithm until better convergence solves the problem <em>eventually</em> — however I would in normal use cases recommend using classic regularization techniques and/or dropout mechanism.</p><p><code>Or</code> and <code>Xor</code> could be learnt more accurately without reusing <code>Not</code> or <code>And</code> — The error rate might explode after stacking the models sequentially, even if the error rate is very low. You can notice that the error rate of <code>Xor</code> with pre-trained <code>Not</code>, is million times larger compared to <code>Not</code>.</p><ul><li><code>Not: 10^{-8}</code></li><li><code>And: 10^{-4}</code></li><li><code>Or: 6 * 10^{-3}</code></li><li><code>Xor: 10^{-2}</code></li></ul><p>This means <em>sufficient</em> pre-training is very crucial not only for the model itself, but also for future models!</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*QZeAeaFA7t6RamiL_mJB8g.png" /><figcaption>Figure 10: Revisiting Knowledge Transfer Across Different Domains</figcaption></figure><h4>Conclusion</h4><p>All in all, knowledge transfer has far-reaching implications, especially in fields like machine learning. Neural networks, as universal function approximators, possess the ability to learn not just from data but also from <strong>other models</strong>. The reusability of pre-trained neural networks lies in their model parameters, easily applied to more complex models. Fine-tuning, the process of training a model with <strong>pre-existing ones</strong> for specific tasks, proves remarkably efficient compared to building models from scratch. The exercise of predicting boolean gates vividly demonstrates the advantages of reusing pre-trained models over starting anew.</p><p>Ultimately, we stand on the shoulders of those who came before us, and this principle guides our progress, even if subtly.
