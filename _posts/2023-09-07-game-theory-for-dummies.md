<figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*SsIXH8Y2NN1_9OAONy5hfQ.png" /><figcaption>A social network as a multi-agent system, Source: <a href="https://ai.vub.ac.be/learning-in-multi-agent-systems/">https://ai.vub.ac.be/learning-in-multi-agent-systems/</a></figcaption></figure><p>What do Wikipedia authors, soccer players, and restaurant staff have in common? No, it’s not entertainment. Yes, it’s the concept of multi-agent systems. These systems capture interactions among diverse actors, each with their own agenda. Whether it’s crafting knowledge, chasing victory, or delivering meals, every actor plays a unique role in such systems.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*0RXB2pfTJ3SQP8tQvgCD4g.png" /><figcaption>Centralized systems vs. multi-agent systems with autonomous agents, Source: <a href="https://www.resilio.com/blog/whats-the-difference-between-peer-to-peer-and-client-server">https://www.resilio.com/blog/whats-the-difference-between-peer-to-peer-and-client-server</a></figcaption></figure><p>In <a href="https://planning.wiki/guide/whatis/aip">centralized planning methods</a>, a central algorithm holds complete control over all agents and determines the best actions for them, given that agents are <em>mere components of the central algorithm</em>.</p><p>However, as agents become more complex with their own beliefs, goals, and so forth, planning requires a much <em>deeper analysis of the social interactions</em> among agents.</p><p>Take a <a href="https://www.robocup.org/domains/1">soccer match</a>, for instance. The home team’s striker wants to score goals, while the rival’s goalkeeper aims to block them. Such <em>conflicting interests</em> are at the core of multi-agent dynamics.</p><p>In such cases, the central controller falls short in considering all available information, also the necessity of <em>more self-governing, decentralized models</em> arises.</p><p>In <a href="https://www.aiforanyone.org/glossary/multi-agent-system">multi-agent systems</a>, achieving harmony is like conducting an orchestra. The chef does not force the musicians to play the music as in planning, she <em>incentivizes</em> each musician to play their own parts. Amid clashing goals and self-interest, the concept of social welfare emerges. Here, individual goals blend into a collective purpose, often diverging from personal desires. It’s about orchestrating desires to create <em>a sense of unity</em>.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*MYhmkDUASE9kXyOp5GZ2nw.png" /><figcaption>Hands forming a circle, Source: <a href="https://www.dnaindia.com/analysis/column-inner-truth-now-is-the-time-for-harmony-2259787">https://www.dnaindia.com/analysis/column-inner-truth-now-is-the-time-for-harmony-2259787</a></figcaption></figure><p>Soccer games, collaborative writing, and bustling restaurants all distill into multi-agent systems, where <em>personal ambitions intersect and collide</em>. It’s a realm where chasing individual dreams must harmonize with the bigger picture.</p><p>In this series, we embark on a journey through multi-agent systems and resource allocation. We’ll decode utility, preferences, and equilibrium, unveiling the fabric of social interactions. Also, we navigate the world of actors and ambitions, uncovering the math that guides us in diverse scenarios.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*TfBGqpRfEehmbZCh3fKyKw.png" /><figcaption>Resource Allocation Problem illustrated as “who gets the pizza?”, Source: <a href="https://www.erezetal.com/research/resource-competition-and-diversity/">https://www.erezetal.com/research/resource-competition-and-diversity/</a></figcaption></figure><h4>Agents’ Interests</h4><p>Before delving into resource allocation, it’s crucial to understand that every multi-agent system unfolds along a spectrum of cooperation, partially self-interested actions, competition, and hostility. While there are scenarios that seem clear-cut, such as:</p><ul><li>a group of cooperative agents working harmoniously, self-motivated by the immediate rewards that come from putting in hard work.</li><li>a clash of competitive agents, striving to maximize individual gain in a multi-player game, with hostility arising when one’s gain spells another’s loss.</li></ul><p>In reality, life isn’t that simple. More often than not, <em>a natural blend</em> of cooperation, self-interest, competition, and hostility takes place. Also, such things as <a href="https://www.investopedia.com/terms/c/coopetition.asp#:~:text=Coopetition%20is%20the%20act%20of%20cooperation%20between%20competing%20companies%20by,producing%20complementary%20or%20related%20products.">coopetition</a> and <a href="https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/frenemy">frenemy</a> exist.</p><h4>Resource Allocation</h4><p>Basically, <a href="https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4613-0303-9_14#:~:text=The%20resource%20allocation%20problem%20seeks,cost%20incurred%20by%20the%20allocation.">resource allocation</a> is all about sharing resources among different tasks and individual agent-resource pairs, e.g. if an agent takes over a task, an edge between agent <strong>A</strong> and (task) resource <strong>R</strong> is added.</p><p>Alternatively, such problems can be rewritten as <em>reallocation problems</em>, starting with an initial allocation and then allowing the swapping of resources to optimize the allocation’s efficiency.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*bQWlmbackxxXk7Tc1CSg2g.jpeg" /><figcaption>Classification of resources, Created by Yamac Eren Ay (me) using <a href="https://app.diagrams.net/">https://app.diagrams.net/</a></figcaption></figure><p>Resources encompass a diverse range, classified by their divisibility, shareability, and units:</p><ul><li>The contrast between divisible (e.g., money) and indivisible (e.g., tasks) resources forms a fundamental distinction.</li><li>Further, resources can be sharable (e.g., information) or non-sharable (e.g., food).</li><li>Additionally, resource utility might be characterized by multiple units, dividing into multi-unit resources (e.g., horsepower / max speed / acceleration) or single-unit resources (e.g., time).</li></ul><h4>Economics of Game Theory: Utility, Preferences</h4><p>The crux of resource allocation lies in understanding agents’ preferences. <a href="https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/072915/what-utility-function-and-how-it-calculated.asp#:~:text=our%20editorial%20policies-,What%20Is%20Utility%20Function%3F,set%20of%20goods%20and%20services.">Utility functions</a> play a pivotal role in quantifying these preferences and benefits (while keeping them private). The utility function <strong>u</strong> maps bundles of resources to their corresponding utility scores, allowing us to <em>compare different allocations</em>.</p><p>Preferences manifest through strict preference <strong>≻</strong>, weak preference<strong> ⪰</strong>, and indifference <strong>~</strong>. These preferences adhere to the principles of transitivity, reflectivity, antisymmetry, and totality. Monotonicity asserts that <em>more resources lead to higher utility</em>.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*C4l63wSbRopg2iEK0cVg9g.png" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*QCjnw9espgdHEPrGWuVmlw.png" /><figcaption>Left: Utility of a good, illustrated as a graph in economics (https://www.wallstreetmojo.com/utility-function/), Right: Indifference curves for apples and bananas (<a href="https://www.economicshelp.org/blog/glossary/indifference-curves/">https://www.economicshelp.org/blog/glossary/indifference-curves/</a>)</figcaption></figure><p><a href="https://www.investopedia.com/terms/m/marginalutility.asp#:~:text=Marginal%20utility%20is%20the%20added%20satisfaction%20a%20consumer%20gets%20from,used%20to%20justify%20progressive%20taxes.">Marginal utility</a> signifies that the first unit of a resource yields more utility than subsequent units, also <strong>u(x) ≥ (u(x+dx) + u(x-dx)) / 2</strong> for any small difference <strong>dx</strong> and each resource <strong>x</strong>.</p><p><a href="https://www.core-econ.org/the-economy/book/text/leibniz-05-04-01.html">Quasilinear preferences</a> <strong>u(x, y) = v(a*x) + b*y </strong>introduce a logarithmic or root function <strong>v</strong>, so that the marginal utility inequality above is satisfied. This commodity shifts indifference curves as consumption increases without altering their slope. The quasilinear preference widens the horizons of allocation possibilities.</p><p><a href="https://www.wallstreetmojo.com/indifference-curve/">Indifference curves</a> visualize the trade-offs between different goods. When goods are concave, the marginal rate of substitution is considered. Indifference curves increase monotonically and never intersect, with maximum total utility achieved <em>when both goods are equally available</em>.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*nmd9T6xegxEzGzrTZMKaAQ.png" /><figcaption>Complementary vs. Substitute Goods, Source: <a href="https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2017/11/07/substitute-or-complement/">https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2017/11/07/substitute-or-complement/</a></figcaption></figure><h4>Bundles of Resources, Additivity</h4><p>Resource allocation is a dance between individual items and their combined power. We use <a href="https://www.statisticshowto.com/superadditive-function-subadditive/">the following terms</a> to talk about how different bundles of resources work against individual resources:</p><ul><li>Superadditivity (Complementarity) assumes that the bundle of <strong>A</strong> and <strong>B</strong> is more valuable than <strong>A</strong> and <strong>B</strong> individually (<strong>u(A ∪ B) ≥ u(A) + u(B)</strong>). For example, left shoe is useless if the right shoe is missing.</li><li>Subadditivity (Substitution) assumes that the bundle of A and B is less valuable than A and B individually (<strong>u(A ∪ B) ≤ u(A) + u(B)</strong>). For example: buying another pair of white shoes is useless if you already have one.</li><li>Additivity is a very special assumption stating that the utility of the bundle is equal to the sum of individual utilities (or alternatively, the resources <strong>A</strong> and <strong>B</strong> are both complementary and substitute).</li></ul><p>However, here’s a twist: even if we know how <strong>A</strong> relates to something else (let’s say <strong>C</strong>), and how <strong>B</strong> relates to the same thing <strong>C</strong>, we can’t always figure out how <strong>A</strong> and <strong>B</strong> relate to each other. It’s like knowing who <strong>A</strong>’s BFF is and who <strong>B</strong>’s BFF is, but not being sure if <strong>A</strong> and <strong>B</strong> are BFFs themselves.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*WVHER3OwYMy2uKc8IH5z7A.jpeg" /><figcaption>Money as a transferable utility in various forms, Source: <a href="https://www.cashtechcurrency.com/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-accept-different-payment-methods">https://www.cashtechcurrency.com/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-accept-different-payment-methods</a></figcaption></figure><p>With money as transferable utility, agents can engage in mutually beneficial exchanges, reshaping outcomes. For example, the one can use money as a way of compensating the financial damage of the other who undersold a complementary good.</p><p>This can make the utility changes more fair and even <em>slightly closer to additive</em>. However, the complexity of such exchanges often leads to <a href="https://xlinux.nist.gov/dads/HTML/nphard.html">NP-hard</a> problems, requiring sophisticated solutions.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*GeWtDjxQ3TYop59OEKZr-A.png" /><figcaption>Utilitarian vs. Egalitarian Social Welfare, Source: <a href="https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/15650/1/15650.pdf">https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/15650/1/15650.pdf</a></figcaption></figure><p>Collective utility functions offer insights into the overall welfare of a society. <a href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330098338_Egalitarianism_utilitarianism_and_the_Nash_bargaining_solution">Social welfare orderings</a> <strong>SWF</strong> assess and compare different utility configurations. Various social welfare functions reflect <em>different societal goals</em>:</p><ul><li>Utilitarian social welfare maximizes the total sum of all individual utilities, and thus it may ignore the <em>unhappiness of individual agents</em>.</li><li>Egalitarian social welfare prioritizes <em>fairness by maximizing the minimum</em> of all individual utilities, but it is most favorable only for agents who are in a very unhappy state.</li><li><a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0176268023000241#:~:text=The%20Nash%20institution%20is%20considered,goals%20of%20efficiency%20and%20fairness.">Nash product</a> ( = the product of all utilities) offers a compromising alternative between the both extremes, balancing shared utility and inequality. Alternatively, the term can be logarithmized.</li></ul><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*ANH8z1I7s8zlk07TRzIR5w.png" /><figcaption>Prisoners’ Dilemma as one of the most widely known problems, Source: <a href="https://www.britannica.com/science/game-theory/The-prisoners-dilemma">https://www.britannica.com/science/game-theory/The-prisoners-dilemma</a></figcaption></figure><h4>One-Shot Games</h4><p>Now, let’s enter <a href="http://pespmc1.vub.ac.be/PRISDIL.html">Prisoners’ Dilemma</a>: Imagine two individuals, both under investigation, confined to separate rooms with no means of communication. They face a crucial choice: remain silent or betray their crime partner. If both choose silence, they each receive a one-year sentence, not bad at all. Should one betray the other, the betrayer goes free while the other faces a hefty 20-year term. Opting for betrayal by both leads to a 5-year sentence for each.</p><p>In an ideal world, <em>mutual trust</em> would make the one-year sentence bearable. However, <em>a discomforting doubt</em> emerges: what if the other person betrays me? Fearing the worst, both prisoners often opt to betray, safeguarding themselves from the possibility of an interminable 20-year sentence. Consequently, both confess to the crime, implicate themselves, and the police investigation wraps up with apparent success.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*Z31nftwbNlpzWIx1J2iVNQ.png" /><figcaption>Abstract representation of a one-shot game (with utility of individual actions), Source: <a href="https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Payoff-matrix-of-the-one-shot-PD-game_fig1_228464490">https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Payoff-matrix-of-the-one-shot-PD-game_fig1_228464490</a></figcaption></figure><p>Formally speaking, we encounter <a href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/game-theory4.htm">one-shot games</a>, characterized by a fixed resource set. Goods are not renewable or dismissible, and strategies are executed simultaneously. Each player makes strategic choices independently, a priori.</p><p>Games in normal form encapsulate the essence of one-shot games, offering a complete information framework. Yet, practicality requires assumptions such as <em>immediate payments and strong rational players</em>.</p><h4>Rational Players, Pure / Mixed Strategy</h4><p>You can think of a strong rational player as someone who plays a dominant strategy. A <a href="https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/capital-markets/dominant-strategy/">dominant strategy</a> is also a go-to move, which would yield the best outcomes, guessing what other players would play.</p><p>But in case of an unpredictable situation, going for a mixed strategy is a savvy move. By way of introducing an element of randomness to the choices, players can estimate their odds and anticipate the potential outcomes. Take Prisoners’ Dilemma: If one prisoner is equally likely to stay silent or betray (50%-50%), the optimal strategy for the other prisoner would be to allocate their moves in the same 50%-50% fashion as well.</p><p>In a nutshell, being a strategic player involves using your intellect to pick the best moves when you can predict them (pure strategy), and injecting a dose of adaptability when things get uncertain (<a href="https://www.theprofs.co.uk/library/pdf/mixed-strategy-game-theory-examples.pdf">mixed strategy</a>). It’s all about making informed decisions and using smart tactics to stay ahead in the game.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*wCQIGblry4SFA49GJ6vEZA.jpeg" /></figure><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*3iz29DYTQOjAxC2DnFBIAg.png" /><figcaption>Left: Pareto Frontier illustrated as the graph containing all Pareto optimal solutions (https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/dominant-strategy), Right: Rock Paper Scissors as a game without Nash Equilibrium (https://kevinbinz.com/2015/06/07/nash-equilibria/)</figcaption></figure><h4>Pareto Efficiency vs. Nash Equilibrium</h4><p>Now, imagine a situation where no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. That’s the essence of <a href="https://www.investopedia.com/terms/p/pareto-efficiency.asp#:~:text=Pareto%20efficiency%20is%20when%20an,can%20move%20toward%20Pareto%20efficiency.">Pareto efficiency</a>. It’s like finding a way to distribute goods so that <em>no one can get more without taking from another</em>. Think of it as reaching a point where everyone’s as happy as they can be, without causing anyone else to be less happy. It dictates that resources cannot be left unassigned, and <em>fairness hinges on rationality</em>.</p><p>Back in the day, there was a notion that if players always made the most rational moves, they would end up with Pareto optimal solutions. But then, along came the famous mathematician <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Forbes_Nash_Jr.">John Nash</a>, who showed us that there are other steady outcomes, which are not Pareto efficient, known as <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash_equilibrium">Nash equilibrium</a> (named after him, fancy!).</p><p>Recall that both prisoners get a one-year sentence if they remain silent and 5-year sentence if they both turn on each other: It turns out that the former is Pareto efficient, and the latter is just Nash equilibrium!</p><p>Formally, Nash Equilibrium is a superset of Pareto Efficiency. It’s when no one can change their strategy to get a better outcome, knowing what others are up to and ensuring that they keep their strategy. In other words, it’s a more specific scenario where <em>everyone’s playing their smartest move, knowing what’s on the table</em>.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*l009bHAHBloc1R-rIiGsZQ.png" /><figcaption>A game tree where the best moves at each sub-game are marked with red and ARAA is the subgame perfect equilibrium, Source: https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Subgame-Perfect-Equilibria-of-Sequential-Matching-Kawase-Yamaguchi/e233b11a371a8c423d4f671f4c20900d4ce52f6c</figcaption></figure><h4>Analyzing Subgames and Induction</h4><p><a href="https://policonomics.com/lp-game-theory1-extensive-form/">Extensive form games</a>, characterized by perfect information and sequential order, provide a deeper look into strategic interplay. Game trees represent players, actions, histories, and payoffs, giving structure to decisions of agents.</p><p><a href="https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-12-economic-applications-of-game-theory-fall-2012/114453d67d6fd1a1d72ecedb29bd88ef_MIT14_12F12_chapter11.pdf">Subgame perfect equilibria</a> set the bar for rational play, demanding optimal choices in any situation. Such equilibrium can be established using backward and forward inductions, which guide agents through complex decision trees, ensuring their strategies align with this elevated form of equilibrium.</p><p><a href="https://thebusinessprofessor.com/en_US/economic-analysis-monetary-policy/backward-induction-definition">Backward induction</a> starts from the bottom, the leaf nodes, and moves upward, figuring out each subgame-perfect strategy along the way, to the root node. At the end, backward induction delivers the final answer for subgame perfect equilibrium, as we follow the path of those marked lines, starting right from the root node and moving all the way to the end.</p><p>In this article, we will skip <a href="https://economics.sas.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/filevault/event_papers/JR01212010.PDF">forward induction</a> to keep this article short enough, as it involves a bit more complexity.</p><figure><img alt="" src="https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/1400/1*YSvXWmFP30EEBstmsOtKGg.jpeg" /></figure><h4>Final Thoughts &amp; Acknowledgements</h4><p>So
